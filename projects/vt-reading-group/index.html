Augmenting Human Cognition with Computing Forums

I started Papert Lab after observing the lack of interest among many academics, researchers, and students in exploring the potential of computers—particularly artificial intelligence systems—in augmenting human cognition and problem solving. As cognitive scientists, HCI researchers, students, and academics, we are uniquely positioned to design AI systems that improve how we learn, work, research, and communicate. Yet, as I become more involved with academia and higher education, I noticed faculty and students looking for the next paper, degree, grant, or promotion without considering the broader implications on humanity; I am diligently avoiding this troubling mindset. If we call ourselves “human-computer interaction” researchers, we cannot fail to address one of history’s most transformative technologies.

If we want to course correct and ensure we focus at least some of our efforts on researching important problems in using AI to augment human cognition, the objective of Papert Lab’s research, I believe there are two parallel actions we can concretely take:

  1. Studying past inflection points of fields deciding to pursue important questions (rather than more trivial ones), how they were approached, and the motivations of the researchers who chose to tackle the complex problems.
  2. Creating an intellectually vibrant milieu (the culture contained in your unique set of connections) of academics, students, and practitioners who relentlessly explore the unglamorous and non-trivial topics lying at the intersection between artificial intelligence and human cognition.

I feel that these two actions progressively strengthen the depth of knowledge of the other, and as such, we must design milieus that reward both of these activities. While the first involves studying, synthesizing, and discussing history, the other requires one to translate their impression of how previous great work was done, and build a community (e.g. lab, discussion group, collaborators, etc.) who are eager to explore new scientific paradigms. While not all novel research must lie at the intersection of technology and cognition, this remains my focus, and will shape Papert Lab’s milieu.

This summer, I am a visiting scholar at Virginia Tech (with the Center for HCI) researching new paradigms in teaching students to interact and build with AI coding assistants. While I’m here (and onwards), I find it important to dedicate a considerable amount of time (Richard Hamming, who did “great research” with Claude Shannon at Bell Labs, would spend every Friday afternoon exploring the grand topics in his field), perhaps between five and ten hours each week, exploring the broader implications of my work. For example, I am currently exploring AI systems that augment human cognition through constructionism, scaffolded metacognition, and interactive scenarios. I also situate my current research in the context of these future paradigms, and take notes on thought leaders like Andy Matuschak, Michael Nielsen, Seymour Papert, and Douglas Engelbart, all researchers who have done great work at the intersection of computers and human cognition.

To do this, Papert Lab is hosting weekly forums on augmenting human cognition with artificial intelligence. Through this initiative, members will explore current and historical analogs to augmenting human cognition with computers and write thoughtful reflections and calls for research through scholarly essays (e.g. 1,000–5,000 word manuscripts on Substack). Most importantly, however, we will research and design novel interfaces for LLMs to foster inquisitive dialogue with humans to enable us to tackle bolder problems. Members from any field will be encouraged to explore the implications of their existing research on developing “tools for thought” with artificial intelligence, and will collaborate with other students and faculty to develop systems that support the realization of these designs.

If you’d like to contribute to our first forum, a link will be posted shortly.

We are initially limiting attendance to six attendees, and may increase spots as we gauge interest.
